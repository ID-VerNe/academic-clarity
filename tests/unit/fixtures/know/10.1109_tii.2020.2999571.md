# A Graph Theory-Based Approach to Detect False Data Injection Attacks in Power System AC State Estimation  
Mohsen Jorjani \(①\) , Hossein Seifi \(①\) , Senior Member, IEEE, and Ali Yazdian Varjani \(①\) , Member, IEEE  
Abstract- Nowadays, an energy management system (EMS) located at power grid control centers plays a critical role in ensuring the reliable operation of a grid. The SE serves as the main interface of EMS with the power grid, and because of that other EMS functionalities rely on its provided data. Recent studies show that the state estimator is vulnerable to various types of cyber- attacks. Among different types of such attacks, the false data injection attacks (FDIAs) are proven to be one of the most stealth ones and attracted much attention. Various countermeasures have been proposed to detect such kind of attacks. However, these approaches are generally based on some assumptions, such as being limited to detecting a specific type of FDIAs or the use of dc formulation for modeling the attack. In this article, a new algorithm is proposed to detect the FDIAs on ac state estimation. This algorithm applies outlier detection techniques on the state estimation results and then detects the possible attack by a graph- theory- based approach. The performance of the proposed algorithm is evaluated by simulating two main types of FDIAs in the IEEE 14 bus and 118 bus test systems. The results show that the algorithm performs satisfactorily in detecting both kinds of FDIAs with high rates.  
Index Terms- Cyber- attack, false data injection attack (FDIA), graph theory, outlier detection, state estimation.

In order to achieve the reliable and secure operation of the power grid, different measurements showing the grid condition are usually gathered from each substation and transferred to the control center, wherein these data are processed by the so- called energy management system (EMS) [1]. EMS consists of different functionalities, which are aimed to be supportive tools for the grid operator by helping him/her deciding to do appropriate corrective and preventive

The power grid states resulting from the state estimator are used as the base data to run other EMS functionalities like contingency analysis (CA), optimal power flow (OPF), etc. Therefore, state estimator security is essential for maintaining the secure operation of the grid.  
So far, numerous researches have been conducted on the vulnerability of state estimators to cyber- attacks [2]. One of the most stealth and destructive types of cyber- attacks on the state estimation process is the so- called false data injection attack (FDIA) [3]. In this type of attack, the attacker injects false data into the grid measurements in order to achieve his goals. This is done in such a way that the conventional bad data detector module located at the grid control center cannot detect the measurements manipulation. Recently proposed FDIA construction methodologies use different assumptions to construct the attack vector. These assumptions can be classified into two main categories, namely, considering the dc [4]- [7] or ac [8]- [11] formulation for the state estimator, and considering whether the attacker has complete information about the grid topology and measurements [4], [9], [10] or his information about them is incomplete [5]- [7], [8], [11].  
Various methods to detect and defend against FDIAs have been proposed in the literature. These methods can be classified into two main groups [12]. The first group includes the approaches that are focused on protecting a set of existing measurements or adding some new secured ones to the grid in order to defend against the attacks [13]- [17]. The major drawback of these kinds of approaches is that the protected measurements cannot always be assured to remain secured. For instance, the PMU data were assumed to be secure in the past, but recent studies show that they are also vulnerable to attacks [18]. So, there is always a chance that the attacker can penetrate the security measures provided for these measurements and put the state estimator in danger.  
The second group of defending methods focuses on the approaches, which make the use of mathematical tools (e.g., statistical or machine learning approaches) to detect the attack or make it harder for the attacker [12], [19]- [28]. However, the previously proposed detection methods falling into this group have also certain limitations. Most of these approaches are formulated and tested based on the dc formulation of the state

estimator [12], [21]- [26]. However, there is no guarantee that these methods work well under the ac formulation, which is typically used in the power grid control centers [9], [20], [28]. This is due to the nonlinearities incorporated in the ac model, which allow the attacker to construct a sparse- based attack by using one of the coexisting spurious solutions of the ac SE problem [9]. In [20], it is shown that using the dc FDIA detection methods (such as the one presented in [24]) to detect ac FDIAs leads to mediocre detection accuracies.  
In order to overcome the limitations of dc FDIA detection methods in dealing with ac FDIAs, some techniques have been proposed focusing on detecting these kinds of attacks. In [19], the Kullback- Leibler divergence (KLD) measure is used to compute the distance between the probability distributions of measurement variations before and after the attack. If the calculated distance is greater than a predefined threshold, the attack will be detected. In [27], the authors propose a moving target defense (MTD) strategy to defend against the ac FDIAs. Although these methods investigated the defense against FDIAs under ac formulation, they just focused on the attacks in which the attacker has complete information about the power grid. Assuming this, the attack can lead to significant changes in the power grid state variables. Recently proposed attacking strategies [11] revealed the possibility of attacking the grid with incomplete network information through manipulating a limited number of measurements inside a specific region. Recent studies show that the methods mentioned above are not suitable for detecting such kinds of attacks [20]. In order to overcome this limitation, a deep neural network based detection method is presented in [20] to detect FDIAs in which the attacker has complete or incomplete information about the grid under the ac condition. However, successful detection of FDIAs by using this method requires the state variables (bus voltage magnitudes and phase angles) to be available at high sampling frequencies (e.g., under \(100\mathrm{ms}\) ). Considering the fact that the state estimation function usually runs with \(1.5 - 5\mathrm{min}\) time intervals in modern power grid control centers [12], it is clear that the data required by the algorithm to work correctly are not readily available. This limits the use of this algorithm in today's power grid control centers.  
In this article, a novel method is proposed to detect and localize ac FDIAs. The proposed method is based on the graph- theoretical analysis of the power grid. The power grid can be considered as a network of buses and branches, which are connected together to deliver the produced electricity to the end- users. Therefore, the entire grid can be represented by a graph, considering the buses as its vertices and the branches as its edges. Some of the previously published papers utilized this concept to deal with the FDIA defending problem. For instance, [13] and [17] model the power grid as a graph and solve the problem of finding an optimal set of protected measurements as a Steiner tree problem. However, as discussed before, these protection- based approaches cannot be fully trusted. In [28], the concept of graph Fourier transform is applied to a graph that is constructed from the estimated power grid states based on the grid topology. Then, the attack is detected by passing the result through a high pass filter. Although this method shows acceptable performance in detecting ac FDIAs with complete information, it does not provide good results in dealing with  
ac FDIAs with incomplete information, as will be shown in Section IV.  
Unlike the ideas, which have been used by the abovementioned approaches, the main idea of the proposed method in this article is based on the fact that due to the power flow equations, the attacker needs to change the measurements obtained from the target bus (or branch) and some of its adjacent branches or buses to ensure a successful attack. First, the probability distribution of the estimated state variables and measurements variations will be used to detect the outliers in the current state estimation results. Then, the proposed method looks for adjacencies in identified outliers by constructing a graph with them. The attack is detected and localized if there is enough evidence that the identified outliers are adjacent and related to each other. This particular procedure of analyzing the detected outliers based on the graph theory concepts makes the proposed method capable of satisfactorily detecting both major kinds of ac FDIAs (ac FDIAs that are constructed with complete or incomplete information about the power grid), as will be shown in Section IV. This can be considered as the main contribution of this article.  
The rest of this article is organized as follows. Section II presents a brief background on power grid state estimation, bad data detection, and false data injection attacks. Section III focuses on the proposed FDIA detection algorithm and explains its basic principles. Section IV presents the results of detecting FDIAs for the IEEE 14 bus and 118 bus test systems by using the proposed algorithm. Finally, Section V concludes this article.  
## II. BACKGROUND  
### A. Power Grid State Estimation and Bad Data Detection  
The operation of the power grid is usually done by monitoring the value of voltage magnitude, phase angle, and power injection at buses and the power flow through transmission lines. The Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) system gathers these measurements and delivers them to the control center. However, these measurements might have errors. These errors are usually due to the inaccuracy of measurement devices, noise in the communication lines, etc. In order to deal with these errors, the raw measurements are first processed by the state estimator. The objective of this process is to use the redundancy in the measurements to find an estimate of the system state vector (bus voltage magnitudes and phase angles, except for the reference bus, which is considered zero) that best fits the measurements. This is done by taking the nonlinear relationship between the measurements and the state variables into account. Based on the ac power flow equations, this relationship can be expressed as follows:  
\[\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{h}\left(\mathbf{x}\right) + \mathbf{e}. \quad (1)\]  
In the abovementioned equation, \(\mathbf{z}\) , \(\mathbf{x}\) , and \(\mathbf{e}\) represent the measurements vector, the state vector, and the measurements errors vector, respectively. Various methods are proposed to find the estimated system state vector \((\hat{\mathbf{x}})\) [29]. The most widely used approach is the weighted least- squares (WLS) method. According to this method, the value of \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) can be derived by

solving the following equation:  
\[\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \underset {\mathbf{x}}{\operatorname{argmin}}[\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x})]^T\mathbf{W}[\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x})] \quad (2)\]  
where \(\mathbf{W}\) is a diagonal matrix, and each of its elements is equal to the inverse of the respective measurement accuracy.  
In order to simplify the state estimation problem and avoid the complexities encountered in solving (2), a dc approximation of this problem is commonly used in the literature. With this approximation, the nonlinear dependence of measurements to the system states becomes linear, i.e.,  
\[\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{e}. \quad (3)\]  
Considering the abovementioned equation, if the state estimation problem is solved using the WLS method, the result will become  
\[\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \left(\mathbf{H}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{H}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}. \quad (4)\]  
Due to the errors present in the measuring devices, some measurements may deviate significantly from their respective real values. These measurements reduce the validity of state estimation results and should be removed from the original measurements set. The bad data detection functionality is usually performed after the completion of the state estimation process to detect such erroneous measurements. Various methods are proposed for bad data detection in the literature [29], with the largest normalized residual test as the most widely used one. The residual is defined as  
\[\mathbf{r}_2 = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll}\| \mathbf{z} - \mathbf{h}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\| _2 & \mathrm{for~the~AC~case}\\ \| \mathbf{z} - \mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{x}}\| _2 & \mathrm{for~the~DC~case} \end{array} \right.. \quad (5)\]  
After the computation of the residual value based on the abovementioned equation, its Euclidean norm is compared against a threshold \(\tau\) . The existence of bad data in the measurements will be detected if \(\| \mathbf{r}_2\| > \tau\) . Because the measurements errors are usually assumed to follow a normal distribution with zero mean, it can be proved that \(\| \mathbf{r}_2\| ^2\) follows the \(\chi^2\) distribution. This is used to determine the appropriate value for \(\tau\) .  
### B. FDIA on Power Grid State Estimator  
In recent years, there has been widespread attention to the possibility of conducting FDIA on the state estimator. The study in [4] was among the pioneering studies to investigate the vulnerability of dc state estimator to such attacks. According to [4], the attacker must have complete information about the power grid configuration to launch a successful attack and bypass the bad data detector. Based on the results presented in there, some other studies have been done focusing on the vulnerability of the ac state estimator to such attacks. For instance, the authors in [10] indicate that a sufficient condition for an attack on ac state estimation to remain hidden is that the attack vector be constructed according to  
\[\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{h}\left(\hat{\mathbf{x}} +\mathbf{c}\right) - \mathbf{h}\left(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\right) \quad (6)\]  
where \(\mathbf{a}\) is the attack vector, which should be added to the actual measurements vector (the attacked measurements vector would become \(\mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{a}} = \mathbf{z} + \mathbf{a}\) ) and \(\mathbf{c}\) is a vector representing the deviation  
of system state variables after the attack (the attacked system states vector would become \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathbf{a}} = \hat{\mathbf{x}} +\mathbf{c}\) ). Considering (6) as the attack vector, the residual value after the attack \((\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{a}})\) would become  
\[\begin{array}{rl} & {\| \mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{a}}\| _2 = \| \mathbf{z}_{\mathbf{a}} - \mathbf{h}\left(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathbf{a}}\right)\| _2 = \| \mathbf{z} + \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{h}\left(\hat{\mathbf{x}} +\mathbf{c}\right)\| _2}\\ & {\qquad = \| \mathbf{z} + \mathbf{h}\left(\hat{\mathbf{x}} +\mathbf{c}\right) - \mathbf{h}\left(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\right) - \mathbf{h}\left(\hat{\mathbf{x}} +\mathbf{c}\right)\| _2 = \| \mathbf{r}\| _2.} \end{array} \quad (7)\]  
From the abovementioned equation, it is clear that the residual value is unchanged and so the attack would not be detected by the bad data detector.  
According to (6), it can be seen that the attacker needs to know the required information to construct the \(\mathbf{h}(\cdot)\) matrix for the whole power grid. This means that he must know the topology of the network and the branch parameters. Besides, he needs to know the estimated values of system state variables. It is practically impossible for the attacker to have such perfect information about the power grid and its related parameters. Therefore, recent studies worked on constructing ac FDIA with incomplete network information [8], [11].  
In [11], an iterative approach to build the attack vector using only the information within a specific region of the power grid is proposed. The attack vector is constructed by estimating the angle differences of the branches based on their respective measured power flows. So, there would be no need for the attacker to have any information about the estimated values of the system state variables. The iterative approach for constructing the attack vector based on [11] can be summarized as follows. First, the state variables (represented by \(\mathbf{V}\) and \(\theta\) ) are initialized. These state variables are updated in each iteration based on the results obtained from the previous ones. Then, the attack vector, which contains the real and reactive powers flowing through transmission lines (represented by \(\mathbf{p}\) and \(\mathbf{q}\) ) and the real and reactive power injections at buses (represented by \(\mathbf{P}\) and \(\mathbf{Q}\) ), is computed and checked to be within a predefined limit. If the conditions are satisfied, then the attack vector is constructed; otherwise, the necessary changes in state variables will be calculated by solving the following optimization problem:  
\[\min \sum_{t = 1}^{10}1^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{t}},\mathrm{subject~to~:}\] \[\left[ \begin{array}{l}\Delta \mathbf{p}\\ \Delta \mathbf{q}\\ \Delta \mathbf{P}\\ \Delta \mathbf{Q}\\ \Delta \mathbf{V} \end{array} \right] = \left[ \begin{array}{l}\partial \mathbf{p} / \partial \theta \partial \mathbf{p} / \partial \mathbf{V}\\ \partial \mathbf{q} / \partial \theta \partial \mathbf{q} / \partial \mathbf{V}\\ \partial \mathbf{P} / \partial \theta \partial \mathbf{P} / \partial \mathbf{V}\\ \partial \mathbf{Q} / \partial \theta \partial \mathbf{Q} / \partial \mathbf{V}\\ 0 \end{array} \right]\left[ \begin{array}{l}\Delta \theta \\ \Delta \mathbf{V} \end{array} \right]\]  
\[\begin{array}{rl} & {\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{min}}\leq \mathbf{P} + \Delta \mathbf{P} + \mathbf{S}_1 - \mathbf{S}_2\leq \mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{max}}}\\ & {-\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{max}}\leq \mathbf{p} + \Delta \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{S}_3 - \mathbf{S}_4\leq \mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{max}}}\\ & {-\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{max}}\leq \mathbf{q} + \Delta \mathbf{q} + \mathbf{S}_5 - \mathbf{S}_6\leq \mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{max}}}\\ & {\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{min}}\leq \mathbf{V} + \Delta \mathbf{V} + \mathbf{S}_7 - \mathbf{S}_8\leq \mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{max}}}\\ & {\theta_{\mathrm{br},\mathrm{min}}\leq \mathbf{G}\theta +\mathbf{G}\Delta \theta +\mathbf{S}_9 - \mathbf{S}_{10}\leq \theta_{\mathrm{br},\mathrm{max}}.} \end{array} \quad (8)\]

In the abovementioned equation, \(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{t}}\) \((t = 1,2,\ldots ,10)\) is the nonnegative slack variable which is used to solve the optimization problem. The matrix \(\mathbf{G}\) represents the transition matrix that converts bus phase angles to the angle difference alongside each transmission line.  
As it can be seen from (8), solving the optimization problem to construct the attack ensures that the injected attack vector into the measurements obeys Kirchhoff's circuit laws. This causes the value of the residual to become even lower than the preattack condition. In addition, each of the elements in the attack vector are enforced to be within a certain upper and lower bound. These upper and lower bounds are considered to be equal to the typical values of each element to make the attack harder to detect. These characteristics make the detection of this attacking strategy more challenging for the conventional bad data detectors. However, the detection of this attack has not been addressed in most of the previously published works. Therefore, one of the case studies considered in this article is devoted to evaluating the performance of the proposed detection approach in dealing with this specific attacking strategy.  
## III. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY FOR DETECTION OF FDIAS  
### A. General Concept  
The basic principle behind constructing ac FDIAs, with or without having the complete information about the power grid, is that one or more estimated system state variables should be changed by manipulating the measurements set. For example, if the attacker wants to change the value of the active power flowing through a branch that connects buses \(i\) and \(j\) together, he needs to construct the attack vector so that the estimated state variables at buses \(i\) and \(j\) would become different from their respective actual values. This means that all of the measurements that depend on these state variables should also be manipulated. This condition is forced by the power flow equations, i.e.,  
\[\begin{array}{l}{P_{i} = V_{i}\sum_{j\in N_{i}}V_{j}\left(G_{i j}\mathrm{cos}\theta_{i j} + B_{i j}\mathrm{sin}\theta_{i j}\right)}\\ {Q_{i} = V_{i}\sum_{j\in N_{i}}V_{j}\left(G_{i j}\mathrm{sin}\theta_{i j} - B_{i j}\mathrm{cos}\theta_{i j}\right)}\\ {P_{i j} = V_{i}^{2}\left(g_{s i} + g_{i j}\right) - V_{i}V_{j}\left(g_{i j}\mathrm{cos}\theta_{i j} + b_{i j}\mathrm{sin}\theta_{i j}\right)}\\ {Q_{i j} = -V_{i}^{2}\left(b_{s i} + b_{i j}\right) - V_{i}V_{j}\left(g_{i j}\mathrm{sin}\theta_{i j} - b_{i j}\mathrm{cos}\theta_{i j}\right)} \end{array} \quad (9)\]  
where \(P_{i}\) and \(Q_{i}\) are the real and reactive power injections at bus \(i\) , \(P_{ij}\) and \(Q_{ij}\) are the real and reactive power flow from bus \(i\) to bus \(j\) , \(V_{i}\) ( \(V_{j}\) ) and \(\theta_{i}\) ( \(\theta_{j}\) ) are the voltage magnitude and phase angle at bus \(i\) ( \(j\) ), \(G_{ij} + jB_{ij}\) is the \(ij\) th element of the bus admittance matrix, \(g_{ij} + jb_{ij}\) is the admittance of the branch between buses \(i\) and \(j\) , \(g_{si} + jb_{si}\) is the admittance of the shunt branch connected at bus \(i\) , \(N_{i}\) is the set of bus numbers connected to bus \(i\) , and \(\theta_{ij} = \theta_{i} - \theta_{j}\) .  
From (9) to (12), it can be seen that any change in the value of a state variable will affect a set of measurements. For example, a change in the amount of \(\theta_{i}\) results in changes in \(P_{i}\) , \(Q_{i}\) , \(P_{ij}\) , and \(Q_{ij}\) , where \(j \in N_{i}\) . Therefore, in order to construct a hidden

### B. Detection Algorithm  
Fig. 1 shows an overview of the proposed detection algorithm. The algorithm consists of five main steps that will be described below.  
Step 1: Read SCADA measurements and run SE: In this step, the latest SCADA measurements will be used to run the state estimation. After the successful execution of state estimation, the bad data detector will analyze the derived results. If some measurements are detected to be erroneous, they will be removed from the measurements vector, and the state estimator will be executed again. This process continues until the measurements vector is free from potential bad data. However, it should be noted that according to Section II- B, FDIAs will bypass the bad data detector in this step. At the end of this step, a vector containing the estimated states and measurements values for the current time step will be created. We call this vector \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t\) .  
Step 2: Calculate the variation from the previous sample: After the execution of the state estimator and obtaining \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t\) , the difference between the current and previous samples of \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) will be calculated. The result is called the estimated variables variations vector for the current time step ( \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t = \hat{\mathbf{y}}_t - \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t - 1}\) ). Vector \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{t - 1}\) is read from the historical database, which contains the previous samples of the \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) vector.  
Step 3: Detect outliers in the data: The purpose of this step is to find possible outliers in the estimated variables variations

vector. In this article, the so- called "three- sigma rule" [30], which is one of the most commonly used methods to identify outliers, is used to achieve this purpose. According to the three- sigma rule, almost all of the observations made from a variable falls within the three- sigma distance to the observations mean value. Therefore, any observation that lies outside this distance can be declared as an outlier.  
In order to use the three- sigma rule to detect outliers in the \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t\) vector, we first need to know the probability distribution of each of its elements. Using the previous values of \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) , which are stored in the historical database, a histogram for each of the estimated state variables and measurements variations (each element of the \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}\) vector) is created. This histogram is used to calculate the mean \((\mu)\) and standard deviation \((\sigma)\) of the respective element. After the calculation of these values, any element of the vector \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t\) , which is outside its respective \(\mu \pm 3\sigma\) regions will be identified as an outlier.  
Step 4: Create a graph based on the identified outliers: Some of the elements of the vector \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t\) might be detected as outliers in the previous step. It should be noted that these outliers may be caused by normal load variations in the power grid. So, it cannot be concluded that the grid is under attack only by observing the existence of some outliers in \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t\) . As stated earlier, in order to detect FDIAs by using the identified outliers in step 3, we can use the fact that the attacker needs to change some of the measurements, which are closely related to each other to attack the grid; otherwise, the attack cannot bypass the bad data detector. In order to verify that the identified outliers are compliant with this requirement, a graph will be created by using them. The final decision about whether or not the power grid has been attacked is made by analyzing this graph.  
Consider an undirected weighted graph \(G = (N,E)\) comprising of a set \(N\) of nodes (or vertices) and a set \(E\) of edges. Based on the outliers found in the vector \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t\) in the last step, this graph will be created as follows.  
1) Consider these two sets of variables: a) A1: voltage magnitudes, phase angles, real and reactive power injections at buses; b) A2: active and reactive power flows at both ends of branches. 
2) For each bus of the grid, if at least one of its respective A1-type variables in vector \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t\) is detected as an outlier, add a node representing that bus to the graph. Then, connect this node to itself with a self-edge and assign the weight of this edge equal to half of the number of A1-type variables in the vector \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t\) , which are related to this bus and identified as outliers.  
3) For each branch of the grid, if at least one of its respective A2-type variables in vector \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t\) is detected as an outlier, add two nodes representing the two ends of that branch to the graph [if they are not already added to the graph from step 4-(b)]. Then, connect these two nodes with an edge and assign the weight of this edge equal to the number of

Step 5: Detect the attack by analyzing the graph: After the graph is created, the concept of node degrees [31] is used to measure the importance of each node. The degree of a node in a graph is simply defined as the number of edges directly connected to that node. This definition is extended in [32] to consider the weights of edges in a weighted graph in terms of the node strength \((s_i)\) , which is defined as  
\[s_i = \sum_{j\in N}a_{ij}\omega_{ij} \quad (13)\]  
where \(a_{ij}\) is the \(ij\) th element of the graph adjacency matrix and \(\omega_{ij}\) is the weight assigned to the edge connecting nodes \(i\) and \(j\) together. It should be noted that a self- edge will be counted as two edges in the process of graph adjacency matrix calculation [31].  
After the calculation of \(s_i\) for each node of the graph, the final decision on whether or not the detected outliers in the vector \(\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t\) are caused by a cyber- attack will be made based on the following rule: if the value of calculated \(s_i\) for at least an adjustable number of nodes \((\tau_{\mathrm{nodes}})\) in the graph is greater than a predefined threshold \((\tau_{\mathrm{strength}})\) , the attack is detected and localized. Otherwise, the current sample of data \((\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t)\) will be treated as a normal one and stored in the historical database to be used for the analysis of the subsequent SE results.  
### C. Tuning of Detection Parameters  
Two parameters, namely, \(\tau_{\mathrm{nodes}}\) and \(\tau_{\mathrm{strength}}\) affect the performance of the proposed detection algorithm. Considering the configuration of the constructed graph, as explained in the previous section, these parameters can only take positive integer values and should be tuned to achieve the desired detection accuracy. Therefore, they can be tuned by defining a detection accuracy index and calculating this index under some combinations of the values that they can take. A combination that results in the

![](images/p5_0.jpg)

best detection accuracy will be chosen as the final set for these parameters.  
In this article, these two parameters are separately tuned for each case study by making use of a part of the historical data. The historical database contains the past normal operating conditions of the power grid. A part of this data is selected and used for simulating different attacking scenarios. Then, the concept of "Balanced Accuracy (BA)" is used as the index for tuning the parameters [33]. According to Fig. 3, BA for any combination of parameters values can be defined as  
\[\mathrm{BA} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{TPR} + \mathrm{TNR}\right) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}} +\frac{\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TN} + \mathrm{FP}}\right) \quad (14)\]  
where TP, TN, FP, FN, TPR, and TNR denote the number of true positives, number of true negatives, number of false positives, number of false negatives, true positive rate, and true negative rate, respectively.  
## IV. NUMERICAL RESULTS AND DISCUSSION  
In order to assess the effectiveness of our proposed method in detecting FDIAs, two different case studies are investigated in this part of the article. MATLAB and MATPOWER [26] are used for simulation purposes. The simulations are conducted on a computer with Intel Core i7- 7700 CPU and 32 GB RAM.  
### A. Case Study 1: AC FDIAs With Complete Information  
The objective of this case study is to investigate the performance of the proposed algorithm in detecting ac FDIAs, considering the assumption that the attacker has complete information about the power grid. One of the most applicable methods for detecting such kinds of attacks is proposed in [19], in which the distance between the probability distributions of measurements variations before and after the attack is computed using the KLD measure and used as an indicator to detect the attacks. In this case study, the results obtained from our proposed algorithm will be compared with the ones presented in this reference.  
In order to present comparable results with [19], the same procedure for setting up the simulation environment is followed. The IEEE 14 bus test system is used to run the simulations [34]. The load data from the New York Independent System Operator (NYISO) [35] for 2012 is used for this test system. This load data have 5- min recording intervals for every 11 regions of the NYISO power grid. A summary of the procedure to generate the 5- min interval data of the system measurements and state variables for the IEEE 14 bus test system is presented below [19].   
1) Link each of the NYISO power grid regions to one of the 11 load buses in the IEEE 14 bus test system.  
2) Normalize each region of the NYISO power grid load data to the initial real and reactive load values of its respective linked bus in the IEEE 14 bus test system.  
3) By adding up the new real and reactive power consumption at all load buses in the IEEE 14 bus system, calculate the ratio of the new total real and reactive loads to the corresponding initial values. Then, increase the amount of generation of each generator proportionally.  
4) For each 5-min time interval, run a power flow on the IEEE 14 bus system. Then, construct the measurements vector based on the results. In this article, it is assumed that the measurements taken from the grid are as follows:  
a) voltage magnitudes at all buses; 
b) real and reactive power injections at all nonzero injection buses (buses with load or generation); 
c) real and reactive power flows from both ends of all branches.  
5) Run the state estimation algorithm based on the measurements derived for each 5-min time interval and store vector \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t\) in the historical database.  
In this article, in correspondence with [19], it is assumed that the power grid works fine until the end of November. Then, it will go under attack in December. So, the data gathered until the end of November are stored in the historical database and known to the system operator.  
The attacking algorithm proposed in [10] is used to simulate the FDIA with the assumption that the attacker has complete information about the power grid. As explained in Section III of this article, parameters \(\tau_{\mathrm{nodes}}\) and \(\tau_{\mathrm{strength}}\) must be tuned in order to achieve a satisfactory performance from the detection algorithm. In this case study, the data gathered from the grid in November are used to tune these two parameters. A total of 8640 normal samples are taken from the grid this month. Based on these samples, random attacks, targeting voltage magnitudes, and phase angles of the grid (to make them 0.9, 0.95, 1.05, or 1.1 times their nominal values) are simulated. So, 8640 random attacks on voltage magnitudes and 8640 random attacks on phase angles are simulated. Then, the performance of the proposed approach in detecting between the normal and attack samples is evaluated by considering different combinations of \(\tau_{\mathrm{nodes}}\) and \(\tau_{\mathrm{strength}}\) values. For each of these combinations, the balanced accuracy of the detection algorithm is calculated.

## V. CONCLUSION  
In this article, we proposed a graph- theory- based detection approach to detect false data injection attacks in power systems. This approach was capable of detecting FDIAs on ac state estimation. The proposed approach used historical data to calculate the amount of difference between two consecutive SE results. Then, using these derived variations, the probability distribution of each of the estimated system state variables and measurements would be calculated. After the injection of false data into the system, the amount of system variables variations would be higher than normal conditions. This was used to identify the individual outliers in SE results for the current time step. Since these outliers might be caused by normal load variations in the power grid, the final decision on whether they are caused by an attack or not was made using a graph- theory- based analysis of the detected outliers.  
The performance of the proposed method was assessed by simulating two cases of FDIAs. The first case was focused on FDIAs in which the attacker had complete information about the grid, while the second case considered an attacker who had incomplete information about the grid and could make the attack through a specific region of the power grid. IEEE 14 bus and 118 bus test systems were used to complete the simulations. The results showed the satisfactory performance of the proposed method in detecting both kinds of FDIAs.

There are various possible future research directions for this work. First, the detection performance of the proposed mechanism can be improved by using other outlier detection methods than the applied three- sigma rule. Second, the effectiveness of the proposed method in detecting other types of state estimator cyber- attacks can be examined. Third, the applicability of the proposed method in dealing with power grid topology changes or renewable resources integration can be investigated. This is because the occurrence of such events in the grid may alter the underlying probability distribution of grid variables and cause detection errors. Thus, it may be needed to adaptively change the detection thresholds of the proposed algorithm whenever the occurrence of such events is detected.

Ali Yazdian Varjani (Member, IEEE) was born in Tehran, Iran, in 1965. He received the B.Sc. degree in electrical engineering from the Sharif University of Technology, Tehran, Iran, in 1989, and the M.Eng. and Ph.D. degrees in electrical engineering from the University of Wollongong, Wollongong, NSW, Australia, in 1995 and 1999, respectively.  
Since 1999, he has been with Tarbiat Modares University, Tehran, Iran. He is currently an Associate Professor with the power electron  
ics group and is the Director of the Power Electronics and Protection (PEPLAB) laboratory. His current major research activities are in the areas of digital signal processing applicable in power systems and power electronics. His current academic interests include a variety of research issues associated with "information and communication technology," "information security," and "power electronics" related topics.
